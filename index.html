Third River

Sarasvatī: speech, water, time

How can a river no one sees be a goddess?

Ford 1vāc

Speech

A word, a sentence, a voice: each falls within the meanings of vāc. The dictionary even includes the sound of a drum or a stone.1 Read the word again as a name, and speech becomes someone who can speak for herself.1 In the hymn numbered 10.125 in the Ṛgveda, the voice belongs to Vāc. She tells us what she can do. She can make a person wise, a poet, or a seer.2

“Through Vāc alone, O sovereign, are known the Vedas, […] history, ancient lore, the sciences, the Upanishads, verses, aphorisms, explanations, [and] commentaries.”3

Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa · Week 4
“Through Vāc alone, O sovereign, are known the Vedas, […] history, ancient lore, the sciences, the Upanishads, verses, aphorisms, explanations, [and] commentaries.”
Ford 2soma · vīṇā

The goddess who chose music

The gods send Vāc to the Gandharvas in the hope that she will bring Soma back with her.3 Soma is both the pressed drink offered in Vedic sacrifice and its deity.4 The Gandharvas recite the Vedas to Vāc. The gods make a vīṇā, play it, and sing to her. She turns to the gods.3 Look again at the instrument Sarasvatī usually holds.4 With this story in mind, you might see in it the memory of a choice.

“The gods said: Gandharvas are fond of women: let us send Vāc to them and she will return to us together with Soma.”3

Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa · Week 4
“The gods said: Gandharvas are fond of women: let us send Vāc to them and she will return to us together with Soma.”
Ford 3sarasvatī

Speech is a river

In the Ṛgveda, the same verses that praise Sarasvatī as a flood of water praise her for inspiring thought and song.2 The Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā makes the connection explicit in the words “Vāg vai Sarasvatī,” or “Speech is Sarasvatī”.5 Later, she is widely known as the goddess of learning, the arts, and wisdom.67 Imagine trying to draw a river that could hold everything a person learns. The passage below gives that thought a direction: follow the water to the ocean.

“As all waters meet in the ocean, so all sciences (vidyā) merge in Vāc.”3

Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa · Week 4
“As all waters meet in the ocean, so all sciences (vidyā) merge in Vāc.”
Ford 4tīrtha

Underground

A ford is a crossing place. The word can also mean a place of pilgrimage.4 In the Gujarati Sarasvatī Purāṇa, the river carries a fire that threatens the world down to the ocean. She chooses where to surface and where to vanish, linking lakes, rivers, and holy places. She even outwits a mountain that blocks her way to propose marriage.7 After the 2001 Gujarat earthquake, some local people understood newly appeared springs as her return.7 Their account tells us how they recognized her presence. The geography of ancient riverbeds is a separate question.

Ford 5kāla

Time is a river

In the lecture, the sun draws water from the earth and sends it back as rain. Rivers have their beginnings in celestial waters.4 The word kāla means time and can also mean death or the time of death.1 Try saying the title of this page aloud. By the time you reach the word river, the sound of invisible has passed. I hear the sentence as a whole because I can hold the earlier sounds in mind as the next ones arrive.

“Time flows like a river (kāla-nadī)”8

Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 1.2.3 · Week 4
The Invisible Third River
“Time flows like a river (kāla-nadī)”
Prayāga · Return

At the confluence

vāc · soma · vīṇā · sarasvatī · tīrtha · kāla

At Prayāga, the Triveni Sangam, the Gaṅgā and the Yamunā meet. The Sarasvatī is said to join them unseen.7 I understand Sarasvatī through flow. Water moves on. Speech passes from a voice into a listener’s memory. Knowledge passes between people, and time passes as you read. Her invisibility can belong to the way she is present. You have just followed her in the words of this sentence.

About this design

The horizontal bands follow the Vāstusūtra Upaniṣad’s teaching that horizontal lines have the nature of water.9 The writing takes its dark color from the Yamunā. Gold belongs to Sarasvatī. At the fourth ford the ground darkens and the images cease because the river has gone underground. The page passes from dawn to night because time flows like a river.
